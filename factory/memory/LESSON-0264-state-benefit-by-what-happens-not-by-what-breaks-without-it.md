**Situation:** a benefit sentence was phrased negatively ("without X, Y goes wrong"), which forces the
reader to mentally simulate the absent state before they can extract the actual value X provides — an
extra inferential step that a directly-stated benefit skips.

**Lesson:** state a benefit by what HAPPENS because of it, not by what would go wrong without it. This is
the same underlying principle as "define a thing by what it is, not by what it is not" applied to
benefit/purpose statements specifically — negative framing is a subtler variant of that same anti-pattern,
easy to miss because it looks like normal cause-and-effect prose rather than an obvious negative
definition. The one legitimate exception: a deliberate contrastive pair where the negative sets up a
positive that lands immediately after it in the same beat ("I don't give the answer. I guide with
questions...") — that is a rhetorical device (voice), not an evasive negative definition, and should not be
mechanically flipped.

**Apply next time:** when drafting or auditing a benefit/purpose sentence, check whether it states what
happens (positive, direct) or what breaks without it (negative, indirect); rewrite negative framings to
positive ones UNLESS the negative is the setup half of a deliberate contrastive pair whose positive
immediately follows in the same passage.
